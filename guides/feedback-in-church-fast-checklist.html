Guide 5: How to Stop Feedback in Church: A Fast Checklist for Volunteers

Guide 5. For volunteers and small tech teams.

By Isaac Walton, IDW Entertainment Technology, New York, NY. Updated Nov 20, 2026.

Feedback is a loop: sound from a speaker enters a microphone, gets amplified, and comes back louder until it rings. Stop it fast by lowering the fader on the ringing channel first, not the gain. Then move the microphone or speaker, and only use EQ after placement is fixed.

The one rule to remember

Fader first. Placement before EQ.

If it rings, lower the fader. Then move the mic or speaker. Use EQ last. This keeps you fast, safe, and reversible on a Sunday.

Feedback happening now: the first five seconds

That short list below is the whole emergency plan. Everything after it explains why it works.

  1. Lower the fader on the channel that is ringing, just enough to stop the ring. Do not reach for the gain knob first.
  2. If you cannot tell which channel it is, lower the main fader a little until the ring stops, then breathe.
  3. Keep the level down while you work out which microphone and which speaker are involved.
  4. Do not push other channels up to make up for it. Fix the loop first, then restore the balance.
  5. Make a mental note of when it happened and who was speaking or singing. You will need it at the end of the service.

In the moment, the fader is your friend: it is fast, reversible, and does not disturb the settings the rest of the service depends on. Gain changes everything downstream at once, which is why gain is a soundcheck job, not a live fix.

Why feedback happens

Feedback is a loop. A microphone picks up a voice. The console makes it louder and sends it to a speaker. The speaker plays it into the room, and the same microphone picks it up again. Round it goes, a little louder each time, until one note wins and rings.

Three things feed the loop:

  • Level: how much level you ask for
  • Distance: how close the microphone is to the speaker
  • Direction: how much speaker sound points straight at the microphone

Every real fix changes one of those three. Break any one of them and the ring stops.

Distance and placement come before EQ

The closest microphone wins. A pastor who holds a handheld microphone near the mouth needs far less level than one who holds it at the waist, and less level means more room before feedback starts. Headset microphones help speech for the same reason: they stay close and stay in one place.

Speakers matter too. Main speakers should sit in front of the microphones where possible, aimed at the seats, not the stage. A stage monitor aimed up at a singer is also aimed at that singer's microphone. Turn the wedge a little, move it a little, or lower it a little before you reach for EQ.

Shure guidance on microphone placement makes the same basic point: get the microphone close to the source and keep speaker sound out of it.

Fader vs gain vs EQ: what to grab when

Volunteers mix these up under pressure. Here is the plain difference.

  • Fader vs gain: the fader controls how loud that channel is in the mix. Gain controls how loud that channel is everywhere. Use the fader live. Use gain at soundcheck.
  • Placement vs EQ: placement fixes the cause of the loop. EQ only softens one note in the loop. Move something first, then use EQ lightly if you still need it.

The usual repeat offenders

  • The pastor microphone drifts: a lapel microphone slips, or a handheld drops lower and lower during the sermon, so the volunteer keeps pushing the level up.
  • Choir microphones opened together: several wide open microphones near the speakers give the loop many paths at once. Fewer open microphones means a calmer system.
  • Monitors aimed at microphones: a wedge turned toward a microphone instead of toward the singer's ears.
  • An empty room at soundcheck: a system that behaved in rehearsal rings in the service because levels crept up and people move. Leave headroom.
  • A microphone carried into the seats, or in front of a main speaker, during a testimony or announcement.

Prevention at soundcheck

Most Sunday feedback was decided at soundcheck.

  1. Set gain properly first, at real service level, using the method in the gain guide linked below.
  2. Walk the stage with each live microphone at service level and listen for the start of a ring near the monitors.
  3. Fix what you find by moving and aiming before you touch EQ.
  4. Agree with the pastor and singers where microphones will be held and where they will not be carried.
  5. Note the channels that rang, and where, in the notebook at the desk, so the next volunteer starts with your knowledge.

Ringing out, in plain terms, and its limits

Ringing out means slowly raising the level at soundcheck until a ring just starts, finding that note, and cutting it a little with EQ so you gain some room before feedback. Done gently, it can help. Done hard, it hollows out the sound and speech loses clarity long before feedback is truly beaten. It also only fixes the exact setup in front of you: move the microphone or open two more microphones, and the safe level moves too. Treat ringing out as the last step, after placement and levels are right.

Sunday checklist: feedback, fast

If it rings:

  • Lower the fader on the ringing channel first
  • Identify which microphone and which speaker are involved
  • Move or re-aim the microphone or speaker before reaching for EQ
  • Check for drifted pastor microphones and extra open choir microphones
  • Use EQ cuts last, and keep them small
  • Note repeat feedback spots in the desk notebook

Before the service:

  • Gain set at real service level
  • Walk test done with each live microphone
  • Main speakers in front of the microphones, aimed at the seats
  • Monitors aimed at ears, not microphones
  • Microphone positions agreed with pastor and singers

Questions volunteers actually ask next

Should I turn down gain or the fader first?

The fader first, every time. Lowering the fader stops the ring fast and changes only the balance, so you can bring it back once the loop is fixed. Changing gain mid service moves the room, the monitors, and the stream together and shifts how close every mix sits to feedback.

Why does feedback happen?

Because speaker sound gets back into a microphone and goes round the system again, louder each time, until one note rings. It needs enough level, a microphone that can hear the speaker, and a path between them. Break any one of those, by level, distance, or aiming, and the ring stops.

Why does feedback happen in the same spot every week?

Because the same microphone and speaker are making the same loop. Note the channel, the spot, and the speaker involved. Fix the placement for that spot, not just the level.

Does EQ fix feedback?

EQ can help a little, after placement and levels are right. A small cut at the ringing note during soundcheck can buy some extra room. But EQ cannot fix a microphone sitting in front of a speaker, and heavy cuts damage speech clarity. Move something first, then use EQ last and lightly.

What if I open more microphones for the choir and it starts to ring?

Every open microphone adds another path for the loop. Open only the microphones you need, bring them up slowly, and leave headroom.

The pastor walks into the audience with a microphone. What do I do?

Lower that channel before the microphone gets in front of the main speakers. Bring it back up when the pastor is back on stage. Talk about that walking path at soundcheck.
