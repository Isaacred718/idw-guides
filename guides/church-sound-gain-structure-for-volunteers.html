Church sound gain structure for volunteers

Guide 2 of 3 core guides. Set it before the service. Leave it during the service.

Gain causes more Sunday problems than any other control on the board. Set it too low and the channel sounds weak and noisy when you push the fader up. Set it too high and the channel distorts, feeds back sooner, and leaves you no room to move.

The good news is simple. Gain has one job, it is set once per source, and there is a clear way to set it.

What gain actually does

Gain is the input level control. It sets how strong the signal from a microphone or instrument is when it first enters the console. Everything after it, the EQ, the fader, the monitors, and the stream, works with the level gain gives it.

Think of it in order. Gain first, then processing, then the fader for balance. If gain is wrong, every later step fights it. If gain is right, mixing gets calm and predictable.

This is true on a small analog board and on a digital console such as a Yamaha QL1. The screen may look different. The order does not change. Wireless microphones, including Shure wireless systems, add one more step: set the receiver and transmitter levels sensibly first, then set console gain for what arrives.

How to set gain, channel by channel

  1. Start with the channel fader down and the gain low.
  2. Ask the singer, speaker, or player to perform at real service level. Soundcheck level is often quieter than service level, so ask for the louder, real thing.
  3. Use the PFL or solo button to view that channel alone on the main meter.
  4. Raise the gain slowly until the loudest peaks sit in the healthy upper range of the meter, below clipping. On many digital meters, that means peaks around minus 12 to minus 6 dBFS, with average speech or singing lower than that.
  5. Watch for any clip or overload light. If it lights, turn the gain down a little. Red is not a target.
  6. Turn off PFL, bring the fader up to a normal position, and balance the channel against the rest of the mix.
  7. Repeat for each channel, one at a time.

A useful test: with gain set well, your faders should live in a similar, comfortable range during the service. If one fader is barely up and the channel is still loud, its gain is likely too high. If another fader is pushed to the top and the channel is still quiet, its gain is likely too low.

Why you do not touch gain during the service

Gain does not change one thing. It changes everything fed by that channel at once: the room level, every monitor mix, any in ear mixes, and the stream mix. One gain change in the middle of a song moves all of those together, and it also changes how close the microphone is to feedback.

During the service, mix with faders. Faders change balance without disturbing the rest of the system. If a source truly gets much louder or quieter than soundcheck, a small, careful gain adjustment may be needed, but treat that as the exception, not the method.

Common gain mistakes

  • Setting gain by ear in the room instead of by the meter. Use the meter for gain, your ears for balance.
  • Setting gain while the singer holds back. Always set it at real performance level.
  • Fixing a quiet channel with EQ or effects. Fix the level first.
  • Running wireless handhelds with very different levels and fixing it all at the console. Check the wireless receiver output settings too.
  • Changing gain to fix feedback. Feedback is usually a placement, level, or EQ problem first. Lower the fader, move the microphone, then look at EQ.

Checklist: gain, before the service starts

  • Set gain one channel at a time, with PFL or solo on the meter
  • Use real service level from each singer, speaker, and player
  • Keep loud peaks below clipping, with clear headroom
  • Bring faders to a normal range and balance the mix from there
  • Check wireless microphones at the receiver as well as the console
  • Once the service starts, mix with faders and leave gain alone
  • Note any channel that needed unusual gain, and check its cable, microphone, or source after the service
